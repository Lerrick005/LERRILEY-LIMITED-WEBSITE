The two terms are often used as if they mean the same thing. They do not. The distinction matters because they answer different questions and require different work.

## Data analytics answers "what happened?"

Data analytics is the practice of examining existing data to understand what has occurred. It is descriptive. It looks backward.

The typical outputs are:

- Reports that summarize activity over a period
- Dashboards that show current state
- Comparisons across time, region, product, or segment
- Aggregations that reveal patterns in what has already happened

The questions are concrete and answerable:

- How many orders did we process last month?
- Which region grew fastest this quarter?
- What is our average response time to support tickets?
- Which products have the highest return rate?

The answers come from the data the organization already has. The work is querying, summarizing, and presenting.

## Data science answers "what is likely to happen, and why?"

Data science is the practice of building models that predict or explain. It is forward-looking, or at least it works toward understanding causes rather than describing outcomes.

The typical outputs are:

- Predictive models that estimate future values
- Classification models that categorize new cases
- Causal analyses that isolate the effect of one variable on another
- Experiments designed to test whether a change actually caused an outcome

The questions are more open:

- Given current trends, what demand should we expect next quarter?
- Which customers are likely to stop using our service?
- Does the new pricing change customer behavior, or is the change due to something else?
- What would happen if we changed this one variable?

The answers require more than querying. They require modeling, testing, and often new data collection.

## The practical difference

The two are not in competition. They are sequential in maturity. An organization that does not yet have reliable analytics cannot do meaningful data science, because data science depends on clean, well-understood data. The analytics layer is the foundation; the science layer builds on top.

Data analytics is backward-looking, produces reports and dashboards from existing data, and uses queries and aggregation. Data science is forward-looking, produces models and predictions, and uses statistics, machine learning, and experimentation. The first question is what happened. The second is what is likely to happen, and why. Time to value for analytics is days to weeks; for data science it is weeks to months.

## Which does a business need?

The answer depends on the question the business is asking.

**If the question is "what is happening in our operation?"** — the need is analytics. This is usually the correct starting point, and it is often where the largest wins are, because many organizations do not have a reliable view of their own operations.

**If the question is "what should we expect, and what should we do about it?"** — the need may be data science. This requires that the analytics foundation exists first. Otherwise the science will be built on unreliable data.

**If the question is "how do we prove that this change caused this outcome?"** — the need is experimentation, which sits within data science but has its own methods.

Most organizations need analytics first. Some need data science too. Very few need data science before analytics.

## What this means in practice

When we work with an organization on data, the first step is always understanding what data exists, how reliable it is, and what questions are being asked. That determines whether the appropriate next step is a reporting layer, a modeling effort, or something simpler still.

Data science is powerful. It is not a substitute for knowing what is happening in the business. The order matters.
